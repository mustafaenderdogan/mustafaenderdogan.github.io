AI and rules engines in cargo insurance claims

October 10, 2026 7 min read

Money in a cargo claim is usually lost through documents that don't agree with each other or deadlines that pass unnoticed. The consignee opens the cartons three days after delivery, finds water damage and tells the exporter, who writes to the insurer a week later. By then the period for written notice to the carrier may already have run out. So the value of the file depends less on the damage itself than on getting this chain right.

This kind of preparation is where generative AI really helps. Even so, "let's put AI into cargo claims" isn't much of a plan on its own, since the risk differs from step to step and so does the role AI can safely play. For each step below, the question is the same: does this work belong to AI, to a rules engine or to the claims handler?

Three kinds of work in a cargo file

A cargo file involves three kinds of work, and each needs a different owner:

  • Reading and classifying (AI): extracting the transport mode, package count, gross weight, delivery date and any reservation noted at delivery from the notice and the documents. The documents are often scanned and in English, which is the kind of material language models handle well.
  • Calculating (rules engine): notice deadlines, time bars, the carrier's liability limit and the recovery potential. International conventions define these, and because the calculations are deterministic they are not left to AI.
  • Deciding (authorised person): cover, declinature, payment approval, recovery and litigation.

The workflow has to enforce this split. When declining a claim or approving a payment is open only to an authorised user, and only with a written reason, the model has no way of making that decision by mistake.

1. First notice and the shipment record

A cargo claim usually arrives as an email or a letter from the broker, covering the route, the goods, how many packages are damaged, when they were delivered and what was written on the delivery receipt. A language model can turn that into a structured shipment record. Two points need care here:

  1. Hold the output to a schema. Transport mode, type of loss, dates and figures have to fit defined types, and an answer that doesn't is rejected and requested again. If something isn't in the text, the model shouldn't guess it, and the field stays empty.
  2. Ask targeted questions for missing essentials. "How was the cargo carried?" and "On what date was it delivered to the consignee?" need answers, because without those two no deadline can be calculated. A file still incomplete after two or three rounds goes to a handler.

2. The document chain

The commercial invoice, packing list, transport document (CMR, bill of lading or air waybill), delivery receipt and survey report all describe the same shipment, so the same figure should read the same on each of them. AI classifies the documents and reads the fields, and rules do the comparison:

  • Is the package count the same on the invoice, packing list and transport document? A lower count on the delivery receipt is evidence of the loss itself.
  • Does gross weight differ between documents beyond a small tolerance?
  • Are container and seal numbers identical on the bill of lading and the delivery receipt? An intact seal with missing contents suggests the shortage arose at loading.
  • Does the bill of lading say "said to contain" or "shipper's load and count"? If so, the carrier may have limited its responsibility for the contents.

What these checks produce are findings. The system notes, for example, "there is a 60 kg difference", and the handler decides whether that is a weighing difference or something to investigate.

3. The deadline calendar

A missed deadline is arguably the most expensive mistake in a cargo file. Preserving rights against the carrier starts with written notice given in time, and the periods depend on the transport regime:

  • Road (CMR): apparent damage calls for a reservation at delivery. For non-apparent damage, written notice is due within 7 days of delivery, Sundays and public holidays excepted. If that is missed, the goods are presumed, until proven otherwise, to have been delivered in good condition, and the burden of proof shifts to the claimant (the consignee, or the insurer by subrogation).
  • Sea (Hague-Visby / Turkish Commercial Code): 3 days for non-apparent damage. Missing it again leads to a presumption.
  • Air (Montreal): 14 days for damage. The consequence is harsher here, because without a timely complaint no action lies against the carrier.

The calendar belongs entirely to the rules engine. Date arithmetic, the destination country's holiday calendar and the count of working days left must all be deterministic, and a file two working days from its deadline should move to the human-priority queue automatically. The deadlines for bringing an action go on the same calendar: typically a one-year time bar for road and sea, and a two-year period for air after which the right itself is extinguished. The deadlines and limits in this article are not legal advice and have not been reviewed by a lawyer.

4. Estimating recovery

Once the claim is paid, the insurer seeks recovery from the carrier, whose liability is limited. Under CMR the limit is 8.33 SDR per kilogram of gross weight. For sea carriage it is 666.67 SDR per package or 2 SDR per kilogram, whichever is higher, and by air it is 26 SDR per kilogram. The recovery potential is the lower of the loss amount and that limit.

This calculation also belongs to the rules, with decimal precision and a dated SDR rate, and the rate and rule version should be stored on the file. Where AI helps here is in drafting the notice to the carrier from the fields in the file. The draft only goes out once the handler has approved it.

5. Cover and transfer of risk

The cause of loss can be matched against the policy clauses. Rainwater, for instance, is covered under ICC (A) but not under (B) or (C). The Incoterms rule of the sale matters as well. Under FOB, risk passes to the buyer once the goods are on board the vessel, and under FCA once they are handed over to the carrier, so if the damage happened after that point, the exporter's insurable interest is open to question. These checks speed up the handler's work, but their output should always be a preliminary opinion with clause references. Without a reference, the result stays "uncertain".

Recording calculations and suggestions

Auditability is something to build into an AI-assisted claims process from day one. For a cargo file, record at least:

  • which model and prompt version produced each suggestion,
  • which rule version and which rate date each deadline and limit calculation used,
  • whether the handler accepted, rejected or corrected each suggestion, and every status change on the file.

Keeping the rules up to date

Periods, limits, holiday calendars, document lists and clause mappings vary between insurers and change over time. They belong in versioned rule sets approved by counsel, and every file should record which version processed it.

What to measure in a pilot

Four numbers make a good start for a cargo pilot:

  1. Share of timely written notices to carriers (before / after),
  2. Handler time per file,
  3. Number of follow-ups caused by missing documents,
  4. Recovery potential versus recovery actually collected.

Keep the pilot small, with one transport mode and a few dozen anonymised closed files. With closed files, finding out which deadline was missed and which recovery was never attempted is usually the most convincing result.

Where to start

If only one piece could go into the first release of a cargo project, it should be the deadline calendar. It needs no AI and can be tested on closed files straight away. The AI steps, such as reading documents and drafting the notice to the carrier, can be added once the calendar works correctly.
