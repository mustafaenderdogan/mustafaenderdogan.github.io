AI in Cargo Insurance Claims: What to Automate and What to Keep Human

October 10, 2026 7 min read

In a cargo claim, money is usually lost in two places: documents that don't agree with each other, and deadlines that pass unnoticed. The consignee opens the cartons three days after delivery, finds water damage and tells the exporter; the exporter writes to the insurer a week later. By then the written notice period against the carrier may already have expired. The value of the file lies less in the damage itself than in getting this chain right.

Generative AI is genuinely useful for this preparation work. But "let's put AI into cargo claims" says little on its own. The risk differs at each step, and so does the role AI can safely play. This article walks through the cargo claims process step by step: what can be delegated and what must stay with the claims handler.

The core principle: reading by the model, arithmetic by rules, decisions by people

A cargo file contains three kinds of work, and each has a different rightful owner:

  • Reading and classifying (AI): extracting transport mode, package count, gross weight, delivery date and whether a reservation was noted at delivery from the notice and the documents. Documents are often scanned and in English; language models are strong here.
  • Calculating (rules engine): notice deadlines, time bars, the carrier's liability limit and the recovery potential. These are defined by international conventions and are deterministic; they are not left to AI.
  • Deciding (authorised person): cover, declinature, payment approval, recovery and litigation.

This boundary should be written into the system itself. A workflow in which only an authorised user can decline or approve payment, with a written reason, removes the possibility of "the model decided by mistake".

1. First notice: structuring the shipment

A cargo claim usually arrives as an email or a broker's letter: route, goods, how many packages are damaged, when they were delivered, what was written on the delivery receipt. A language model can turn this into a structured shipment record. Two things matter:

  1. Force the output into a schema. Transport mode, type of loss, dates and figures must fit defined types; non-conforming answers are rejected and re-requested. Information that isn't in the text stays empty rather than invented.
  2. Ask targeted questions for missing essentials. "How was the cargo carried?", "On what date was it delivered to the consignee?" Without these two, no deadline can be calculated. Files still incomplete after two or three rounds go to a handler.

2. The document chain: is the same number the same everywhere?

The commercial invoice, packing list, transport document (CMR, bill of lading or air waybill), delivery receipt and survey report all describe the same shipment. AI classifies the documents and reads the fields; rules do the comparison:

  • Is the package count the same on the invoice, packing list and transport document? A lower count on the delivery receipt is evidence of the loss itself.
  • Does gross weight differ between documents beyond a small tolerance?
  • Are container and seal numbers identical on the bill of lading and the delivery receipt? An intact seal with missing contents suggests the shortage arose at loading.
  • Does the bill of lading say "said to contain" or "shipper's load and count"? If so, the carrier may have limited its responsibility for the contents.

A mismatch is a finding, not a decision. The system says "there is a 60 kg difference"; the handler decides whether it is a weighing difference or something to investigate.

3. The deadline calendar: the most expensive mistake

The first condition for preserving rights against the carrier is timely written notice, and the periods depend on the transport regime:

  • Road (CMR): for apparent damage a reservation at delivery; for non-apparent damage written notice within 7 days of delivery, Sundays and public holidays excepted. If missed, the goods are presumed delivered in good condition and the burden of proof shifts.
  • Sea (Hague-Visby): 3 days for non-apparent damage, again with a presumption as the consequence.
  • Air (Montreal): 14 days for damage, with a harsher consequence: without a timely complaint, no action lies against the carrier.

This calendar is the rules engine's job, not AI's. Date arithmetic, the destination country's holiday calendar and "how many working days are left" must be deterministic, and a file two working days from the deadline should automatically move to the human-priority queue. Time bars (typically one year for road and sea, two for air) belong on the same calendar.

4. Recovery estimate: what is the limit, what is the potential?

After paying the claim, the insurer seeks recovery from the carrier, but the carrier's liability is limited: 8.33 SDR per kilogram gross under CMR, the higher of 666.67 SDR per package or 2 SDR per kilogram for sea carriage, and 26 SDR per kilogram by air. The recovery potential is the lower of the loss amount and that limit.

This calculation, too, belongs to rules, with decimal precision and a dated SDR rate; the rate and rule version should be stored on the file. AI's role here is not arithmetic but drafting the notice to the carrier from the file's fields. The draft always goes out with the handler's approval.

5. Cover and transfer of risk: an opinion, not a decision

The cause of loss can be matched against the policy clause: rainwater is covered under ICC (A) but not under (B) or (C). The Incoterms rule of the sale matters too: under FCA or FOB, risk passes to the buyer at loading; if the damage occurred afterwards, the exporter's insurable interest is in question. These checks speed up the handler, but their output must always be a preliminary opinion with clause references. Without a reference, the result stays "uncertain".

Make every suggestion traceable

In an AI-assisted claims process, auditability is not a feature to add later. At a minimum, record:

  • which model and prompt version produced each suggestion,
  • which rule version and which rate date each deadline and limit calculation used,
  • whether the handler accepted, rejected or corrected each suggestion, and every status change on the file.

Rules belong in configuration, not code

Periods, limits, holiday calendars, document lists and clause mappings vary between insurers and change over time. They should live in versioned rule sets, be approved by counsel, and every file should record which version processed it.

How to measure it

Four numbers make a good start for a cargo pilot:

  1. Share of timely written notices to carriers (before / after),
  2. Handler time per file,
  3. Number of follow-ups caused by missing documents,
  4. Recovery potential versus recovery actually collected.

Keep the pilot small: one transport mode, a few dozen anonymised closed files. In closed files, the answer to "which deadline was missed, which recovery was never attempted" is usually the most convincing result.

Conclusion

AI is most valuable in cargo claims when it turns a scattered, multilingual document pack into a readable file. Deadlines and limits belong to rules; cover and payment decisions belong to people. A system that draws this line correctly from the start reduces lost rights and stands up to audit.
