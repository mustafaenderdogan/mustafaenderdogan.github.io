Preparing motor insurance claim files with AI

October 9, 2026 6 min read

Most of the time spent on a motor claim goes into getting the file ready for a decision. The policyholder describes the incident in two sentences over a messaging app, the date is missing, the other vehicle's plate isn't there, and the accident report arrives three days later. When the claims handler opens the file, a large part of the job is chasing what's missing.

Generative AI is genuinely useful for this preparation work. Still, "let's put AI into claims" doesn't say much on its own, because the level of risk is different at each step of the process and the role AI can take changes with it. The sections below go through a motor claim step by step and look at what can be handed over and what should stay with the expert.

The line between preparation and decision

Accepting from the start that AI will sometimes be wrong makes the design simpler. If the model extracts a field incorrectly, the handler fixes it in seconds. A wrong rejection is a different matter, because it turns into a complaint, reputational damage and regulatory risk. That's why the line is best drawn like this:

  • Can be handed over: extracting information from text, spotting gaps, classifying documents, flagging inconsistencies, suggesting priority.
  • Can support, cannot decide: coverage pre-checks, payment amount suggestions, suspicious-file flags.
  • Authorised person only: rejection, payment approval, recovery and litigation decisions.

The system itself has to enforce this boundary. If only an authorised user can reject a claim or approve a payment, and has to write down a reason to do it, the possibility that "the model made a decision by mistake" goes away entirely.

1. First notice of loss

Most claims now arrive as free text: a message, an email, the description box on a web form. Language models are good at extracting the incident type, date and location from that text, along with whether a third party was involved and whether anyone was injured, which turns the free text into a structured file. Two things matter here:

  1. Make the output fit a schema. Don't let the model answer freely. Every field needs a defined type and a set of allowed values, and an answer that doesn't fit the schema is rejected and requested again.
  2. Don't leave policy matching to the model. Matching on policy number, plate or national ID is a deterministic query against the database. The model's job should stop at reading the text.

2. Filling in missing information

When a mandatory field is missing, the system can ask the policyholder a targeted question instead of sending a generic "please complete your details": "What time did it happen?" or "Do you know the other vehicle's plate?" That alone cuts down the back-and-forth.

The loop needs a limit, though. A file that is still incomplete after two or three rounds should go to a human handler, because leaving a policyholder stuck in a long exchange with a machine is worse than having an incomplete file.

3. Prioritising injury claims

A file involving injury or death shouldn't wait in the same queue as a cracked windscreen. The model usually picks up injury details in the text, but "usually" isn't good enough here. A rule-based second check (phrases such as "injured", "ambulance", "hospital") catches what the model misses and moves the file into a human-priority queue. Moving a file with no injury up the queue by mistake costs a little time, whereas an injury claim left waiting is a serious failure. The check should be built with that in mind.

4. The document assistant

A collision claim and a hail claim don't need the same documents. Generating the required document list for the claim type automatically and sending it to the policyholder in one go is a simple step, and an effective one. Conditions such as extra documents when someone is injured can be defined as rules.

When documents arrive, AI does two things:

  • Classification: is the upload a registration certificate, a driving licence or an accident report? If the wrong document was uploaded, the policyholder is told immediately.
  • Cross-checking: do the plate and chassis number on the registration match the policy and the accident report? Is the date within the policy period?

When something doesn't match, the system reports it as a finding, for example "the plate doesn't match". Whether that is a typo or something to investigate is for the expert to decide.

5. Coverage pre-check and payment suggestion

By reading the policy terms and add-on covers, the system can give a first answer to "does this incident look covered?". It can also calculate a payment suggestion from the assessor's report and the parts-and-labour lines. Both speed up the handler's work. Their output should still always come as a reasoned suggestion that shows which clause and which document the conclusion rests on.

The audit trail

In an AI-assisted claims process, auditability has to be designed in from the start. At a minimum, record:

  • which model and which prompt version produced each suggestion,
  • whether the handler accepted, rejected or corrected it,
  • who changed the file's status, and when.

With this record you can show what happened when a dispute comes in. You can also measure which suggestions are actually accepted and use that to improve the system.

Versioned rule sets

Which fields are mandatory, which questions are asked and which documents each claim type requires vary from insurer to insurer, and even between agencies. If they are hard-coded, every change needs a software release. It is better to keep them in configuration as versioned rule sets. The operations team can then change the process themselves, and each file records which rule version it was processed under.

How to measure it

Whether the AI "worked" should be answered with a few numbers. For a pilot, these three are a good start:

  1. handler time per file,
  2. the number of times the policyholder is contacted again because of missing documents or details,
  3. the acceptance rate of AI suggestions, per field.

Keep the pilot small, with one line of business, one claim type and a few dozen anonymised historical files, and don't expand the scope until the results are clear.

Conclusion

If you are evaluating a product or a project against the steps in this article, the first thing to check is the authority boundary: can the model reject a claim or approve a payment on its own? If it can, fix that first, however good the other features are.
