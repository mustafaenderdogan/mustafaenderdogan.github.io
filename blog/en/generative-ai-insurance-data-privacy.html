Insurance and privacy

Technical privacy measures for generative AI in insurance

October 9, 2026 5 min read

AI projects at insurers tend to slow down at the same point. The technical team wants to try reading claim notifications with a language model, and legal and information security ask, quite rightly, "where does this data go?" That question is part of designing the project properly, and most of the answer lies in the architecture.

This article is not legal advice. On any real project, make sure you work with your legal and data protection officers. The examples refer to Turkey's personal data protection law (KVKK), which was modeled on EU data protection law. Below is my checklist of the technical measures you can take before the data reaches the model and after it does.

1. Draw the data flow first

Saying "we use AI" isn't enough. Show on a single page which data goes to which component, from the moment a claim enters the system until it is archived. The diagram should answer these questions:

  • Where does the data come from? (messaging apps, email, web forms, call centre notes)
  • Where is it stored, and who can access it?
  • What exactly is the text sent to the language model? Do images and PDFs go too?
  • Does the model provider store the data or use it for training? What does the contract say?

The diagram makes the design clearer, and you can use it as the basis for updating your privacy notice and data inventory.

2. Mask personal data before it reaches the model

To understand a claim, the model doesn't need the policyholder's phone number, national ID or IBAN. These fields can be detected in a rule-based layer and replaced with placeholders before the text is sent:

  • The national ID number becomes [ID], the phone number [PHONE], the IBAN [IBAN] and the email address [EMAIL].
  • Names and addresses are masked too, where needed.

Masking should be deterministic, built on regular expressions and validation rules (such as the check digits of a Turkish ID number). Don't rely on telling the model to "mask this". The real values stay in your system, and tasks such as policy matching are done on your side with those values.

Fields such as the policy or insurance certificate number need more thought. The number matters for the claim, but it can also be linked indirectly to a person or a sole trader. Does the model really need to see it, or should the matching happen on your side? Decide this field by field.

3. Convert images and documents to text first

Delivery receipts, survey reports and copies of ID documents can contain signatures and ID numbers. If you convert them to text on your own server (OCR) and run that text through the same masking layer, instead of sending the images to an AI service, you expose considerably less data. The model then sees only masked text.

4. Treat injury information as health data

Something often missed in claims is that a sentence like "the other driver was injured and taken to hospital" contains health data. KVKK, like the GDPR, gives health data extra protection as a special category. In practice, this means:

  • the purpose of processing injury information (for example, prioritising the file) should be clearly defined,
  • medical details (diagnosis, treatment) shouldn't go to the model unless necessary,
  • access to these fields should be limited to a narrower group of roles.

5. Start with cross-border transfer switched off

Many popular language model services run outside the country where the data was collected. KVKK's rules on transfers abroad changed in 2024, and the amendment redefined the rules on adequacy decisions, appropriate safeguards such as standard contracts, and the narrow exceptions that apply only to occasional transfers. Which route fits your situation is a legal assessment.

On the technical side, you can turn that decision into a switch in the system. Use of models hosted abroad starts disabled per insurer or agency and is enabled once the legal review is complete. While the switch is off, the system should keep working with a model hosted in-country or with rule-based steps only.

6. Storage

  • Encryption: claim text and personal fields should be encrypted at field level in the database.
  • Separation: in a system serving several insurers or agencies, each tenant's data should be kept apart, and that separation verified by automated tests.
  • Retention: decide how long raw model-call logs are kept; debugging logs shouldn't contain personal data at all.

7. Audit trail and explainability

Being able to show later what the AI suggested on a file, and what the handler did with that suggestion, matters for internal audit and for any request from the data subject. So every suggestion should be stored along with the model used, the prompt version and the masked input. Status changes on the file should go to a record that can't be altered afterwards.

8. The limit on automated decisions

KVKK gives individuals the right to object when the automated analysis of their data alone produces a result against them. That's a strong reason for decisions with consequences, such as rejection or payment, to be made by a person with a written reason. My own preference is to leave preparation and suggestions to the AI and keep the decision with an authorised person.

Quick checklist

  1. Is the data flow diagram ready?
  2. Are ID, phone, IBAN and email masked before reaching the model?
  3. Do images go through OCR and masking before they reach the model?
  4. Is health data handled separately?
  5. Can the use of models hosted abroad be switched off per insurer?
  6. Are personal fields encrypted and separated per tenant?
  7. Is every suggestion recorded with its model and prompt version?
  8. Do consequential decisions stay with people?

Conclusion

In my view, data protection law sets the frame for a good AI architecture in insurance. When these measures are designed in from the start, the conversation with legal and security moves from "why we can't" to "under which conditions we can".
