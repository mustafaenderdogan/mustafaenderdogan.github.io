Insurance case study

Design decisions behind HasarAI, a cargo claims assistant

October 9, 2026 5 min read

HasarAI is a cargo (goods in transit) claims assistant for insurers, brokers and surveyors. It takes the scattered pack of documents that comes with a cargo claim and turns it into a file that is ready for review, with the deadlines and recovery potential already known. This article covers the main design decisions and the reasons behind them.

The problem

In a cargo claim, the handler's real job is to assess cover and the loss. Before the file gets to that point, though, there is a series of problems to deal with. The notice arrives by email, often in English. The invoice, packing list, transport document and delivery receipt come from different people on different days, and the package counts or weights don't agree across documents. On top of that, the window for written notice to the carrier is only a few days. Most of this preparation is repetitive, rule-bound reading, and working out deadlines and limits is pure rules.

Design principle

The design starts from what the system does not do. HasarAI never declines a claim or approves a payment, and it never uses a language model to calculate deadlines, limits or amounts. The AI is there to read the documents. The rules engine does the arithmetic, and a person makes the decisions. A file moves through these statuses in order: Notice, Awaiting documents, Under review, Survey, Awaiting approval, Payment approved and Closed. Only an authorised person can decline a claim or approve a payment, and only with a written reason.

1. First notice and the shipment record

From a notice in Turkish or English, the system extracts the type of loss, transport mode, transport document number, route, package count, gross weight, damaged portion, delivery date and whether a reservation was noted at delivery. The model's answer has to fit a defined schema, and anything that isn't in the text is left empty. If the transport mode or delivery date is missing, the insured gets a targeted question. A file that is still incomplete after three rounds goes to a handler.

2. Checking the document chain

Documents don't go to the model as images. They are converted to text and masked on the server first, and then classified. The system lists the documents required for the transport mode and type of loss, and checks that package counts, gross weights, container and seal numbers are the same on every document. A shortage in the delivery tally is shown as a separate finding. If the bill of lading has a "said to contain" clause, a rule-based second check catches it even when the model misses it.

3. Deadline calendar

The rules engine works out the notice deadline and the time bar for each regime (CMR, Hague-Visby, Montreal). Under CMR, public holidays in the destination country are left out of the count. For a country with no holiday calendar, only Sundays are left out and the file gets a warning. A file that is two working days from its deadline, or already past it, moves to the human-priority queue. Deadlines are recalculated every morning, so the priorities stay current.

4. Recovery estimate

The carrier's liability limit is calculated in SDR from the damaged kilograms or packages, then converted at a dated exchange rate that the handler enters. The arithmetic is done with decimal precision. The recovery potential is the lower of the loss amount and the limit. For air cargo, a missed notice deadline means the potential is zero. For road and sea, the file records that the burden of proof has shifted to the claimant. Every estimate is stored as a separate record with its rule version and rate date.

5. Data protection and masking

Before any text reaches the model, a rule-based layer masks national ID numbers, tax numbers, phone numbers, IBANs and email addresses, in English documents too. Insured details are encrypted at rest. AI services hosted abroad are switched off by default for each insurer. This is covered in more detail in a separate article on generative AI and data privacy in insurance.

6. Traceability and rules

Every AI suggestion is stored with the model and prompt version that produced it, and every calculation with its rule version. Handlers' decisions to accept, reject or edit a suggestion are measured, and every status change is written to an audit log that can't be altered. Periods, limits, holiday calendars and document lists aren't hard-coded. They sit in versioned rule sets for each insurer, designed to be signed off by counsel.

The in-browser demo

The HasarAI demo (interface in Turkish) runs entirely in your browser on synthetic data, and nothing is sent to a server. You can pick one of three scenarios (wet cartons, a container short-landed, an air shipment with a missed deadline), or enter the regime, delivery date and damaged weight of your own file to see its deadline calendar and recovery limit.

What's next

Two modules come next. One is a cover pre-check that matches the policy clause against the cause of loss and the Incoterms transfer of risk. The other is HasarAI Verify, a second pair of eyes on survey reports. It will check the counted packages against the transport document, the cause of loss and its basis, the packing assessment and the consistency of the depreciation calculation. These modules will also produce findings only, and the decision will stay with a person.

Lessons from this case

  • Settle what the AI will not do before anything else. That is a more important design decision than what it will do.
  • Numbers with legal consequences, such as deadlines and limits, shouldn't be left to the model. They should be calculated by versioned, traceable rules.
  • Don't rely on a single model for important signals. For a clause like "said to contain", a rule-based second check is cheap and effective.
  • The most honest indicators of whether the system really helps are the timely-notice rate and the suggestion acceptance rate.
